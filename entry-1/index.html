about

A lid is often one of the smallest parts of a package, and its main purpose seems simple: to keep what is inside contained and protected. But lids are also surfaces where packaging becomes recognizable. A pattern, a color, a material, or a small logo can change the way a container looks and how we understand the product inside it.

Food packaging is full of these visual decisions. We see them when we walk through a grocery store, open a jar from the refrigerator, or reach into a cabinet for something we have bought many times before. Even when we are not consciously paying attention, packaging helps us identify products and creates expectations about their taste, origin, and quality. A gingham pattern might bring to mind kitchen textiles, homemade food, or family recipes. A metal lid can suggest preservation, practicality, and traditional canning. A bright color can make a product stand out on a crowded shelf or help us recognize a particular flavor. These associations are not created by the lid alone, but the lid can play an important role in communicating them.

My original book explores these details by looking closely at lids and their place in food packaging design. It examines how patterns and materials carry associations with tradition and preservation, how ice cream containers contribute to brand identity, and how color influences the way we understand and recognize food products. Through these examples, the book considers how packaging communicates beyond its practical function and how seemingly small design choices contribute to a product’s identity.

This website is a continuation of that book, offering a new and expanded way to explore the same subject. Rather than simply reproducing the book in a digital format, the website acts as an index and an accompanying informational resource. It provides another way to navigate the ideas introduced in the book, revisit specific topics, and discover information that extends beyond its original pages. While the book presents the research through a printed format, the website creates more space to organize, expand, and connect that information. Together, they form two parts of the same project, each offering a different way to engage with food packaging design.

I became interested in lids because of their relationship to the rest of a package. A label might contain the name of a product, information about its ingredients, or an image that immediately tells us what we are buying. The lid works alongside these elements, sometimes repeating information and sometimes introducing a different material, pattern, or shape. When products are placed next to one another, these surfaces reveal similarities and differences that might otherwise go unnoticed. A jar sitting in the refrigerator, for example, becomes part of the background of a kitchen, even though its design has helped make it recognizable. Looking at the lid separately allows me to examine how its visual qualities contribute to the larger identity of a product.

The website develops this research by making the information easier to navigate and giving individual topics room to be explored further. As an index, it connects the themes and examples from the book, allowing readers to move between them and understand how they relate to one another. As an informational experience, it builds on the original research rather than treating the book as a finished endpoint. The digital format offers a different perspective on the material, allowing information to be revisited, expanded, and understood through a new structure. It also demonstrates how design research can exist across different mediums, with each format contributing something distinct to the overall project.

Ultimately, both the book and the website explore how design decisions influence our experience of everyday objects. A surface does not need a large illustration or a complicated layout to communicate something. The choice of material, the arrangement of a pattern, or the use of a particular color can change how an object is understood. These choices become especially meaningful when repeated across products and over time. I am interested in the relationship between the ordinary and the intentional: objects designed to be opened, used, stored, and eventually thrown away that still carry visual decisions capable of shaping our expectations. The book begins this investigation through printed research and visual examples, while the website continues it by providing a new way to access, organize, and expand the information behind them.
